A small model that decides when a coding agent should ask you first

For each tool call an agent wants to run, the safety adapter predicts whether you would let it go ahead. The agent acts only when that prediction is confident enough. Otherwise it asks. This page covers seven versions of the adapter, the risk rater that feeds it, two clean tests on data nobody had seen, and what happened when the gate ran against 130 real decisions.

How the data flows

There are two halves. Training happens once, on synthetic data: LLM-written tool calls get risk labels from a three-model vote, simulated personas decide on them, and two small LoRA adapters learn from the result on top of one frozen Qwen3.5-4B.

At run time, each tool call the agent proposes passes through four steps in order. The gate either lets the agent act or asks you. Every answer you give goes into a decision log, and the adapter reads your last 12 decisions next time.

Walk a tool call through the gate

Pick an action, a persona and a risk source. The gate rates the risk, checks a short list of commands that always need a person, reads the adapter's P(allow) and compares it with the persona's threshold. High-risk actions need 0.95 or more, and irreversible ones always go to the person.

Switch the risk source to "High as medium" to see the error that matters most. A high-risk action shown as medium skips the 0.95 floor, and that is how risky wrong acts happen.

What the model reads and how it is scored

AIC is a personal decision model. One frozen Qwen3.5-4B carries small LoRA adapters for different roles, and each role is later fitted to one person. The safety adapter is the first role. It reads a persona, that persona's autonomy setting, up to 12 past decisions and the proposed tool call, then scores the options and returns P(allow).

Risky wrong act

The gate acted on a high-risk or irreversible action that the person would have refused. The count that has to stay near zero.

Needless ask

The gate asked about something the person would have approved. Too many and people stop reading the prompts.

Accuracy

How often the adapter's prediction matches the persona's decision. An oracle that knows the true probabilities scores 0.85 to 0.89.

ECE

Expected calibration error. Whether a P(allow) of 0.8 is right about 80% of the time. Lower is better.

Seven versions, one change that mattered

Accuracy moved from 0.72 to about 0.80, but the number that drove each step was risky wrong acts. Through v4, every training change left the model confidently wrong on high-risk actions. Putting the risk level in the prompt in v5 cut those mistakes from 23 to 2.

    Risk level shown in the prompt

    One added line, "Risk (from the rules layer): high", plus risk tags on past decisions. The largest single gain, with no extra threshold. Kept as the base recipe.

    acc 0.80 · ECE 0.020 · 2 risky

  7. v6

    Trained on noisy risk labels

    12% of high shown as medium, 5% of irreversible as high, 10% of the rest shifted by one level. Same clean accuracy, about a third fewer risky wrong acts when labels are badly wrong.

    Consensus risk labels plus noise

    v6's recipe on labels re-rated by two more models against a written rubric. Paired with the rubric risk rater (risk-v7-rub), the 0.95 floor and the always-ask list, this is the chosen gate. Current version.

    acc 0.82 · ECE 0.025 · 6 risky (0 with floor)

Why this is not the end of it

v5 was scored with the risk labels the data generator wrote. In real use nobody hands the model those labels. They have to come from another model, which will sometimes be wrong. The rest of the work is about that gap.

Most remaining errors come from the risk label

Shown a wrong risk level, the adapter trusts it. With no risk line at all, v5 made 69 risky wrong acts on the fresh holdout even with the 0.95 floor, worse than v2. If every high-risk action is shown as medium, v5 makes 63 and v6 makes 44. The adapter copes with mild noise, but not with high-risk actions that are rated low across the board.

Along the way the generator's own labels turned out to be inconsistent. Every action was re-rated by DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Qwen3.6-35B against a written rubric, and the majority vote with the original label became the consensus label.

The rubric in the prompt is what fixed the over-flagging

A second rubric, r2, checks each action step by step from irreversible down to low, with explicit rules for global installs, production build config, force-push, deletes, messages and secrets. Re-labeling the disputed actions with it raised agreement between the LLM labelers from 53% to 70%.

Training on r2 labels alone (risk-v4, risk-v5) made the rater more accurate but not less cautious. A few clearly high-risk actions still scored near zero, so the round-up threshold had to sit very low, and half of the harmless actions got pushed to high. Giving the rater the whole rubric in its prompt (risk-v6-rub) roughly halved that, with no loss in safety.

risk-v7-rub keeps that prompt and trains on about 1,800 more actions from three writer models, each labeled by three raters, for $1.48. On 249 sealed actions it picks the exact level 84% of the time, up from 77% for v6-rub, and lets none of the 93 high-risk actions through below high. It is packaged as one component with pinned settings, so later safety models can reuse it.

Two clean tests, each scored once

The fresh holdout had been used to compare about a dozen pipelines, so its numbers were optimistic. For each clean test the evaluation plan was written first, then a new test set was built from actions and personas nothing had seen, and the frozen pipeline was scored once. After that the set is spent.

Clean test 2 showed the earlier 37% needless asks did not hold. The safety result did. Clean test 3 is the same check with the rubric rater.

What changed

With the rubric rater the gate still made no risky wrong acts, and needless asks fell from 38.1% to 32.7% on the same decisions. That closes about half of the gap to the 27.3% a perfect risk label would give. Calibration error halved, and the gate acts on its own for 21% of actions instead of 15%.

One caveat, and an add-on

Some labeling requests came back incomplete, so 98 of the generated actions had no full label and test 3 ran on the other 174. That is logged in the plan and the result was not re-scored. Once labeled, the left-out actions (86 after removing duplicates) were scored as a separate 2,400-decision test. It agrees: the new rater makes 0 risky wrong acts with 31.6% needless asks, and the previous rater makes 8 with 37.5%.

Shadow mode on 130 real permission decisions

Every tool call approved or rejected at a Claude Code permission prompt between 18 October and 7 November 2026 went through the gate, which scored each one and never acted. There were 128 approvals and 2 rejections across 8 projects. The decision text stays private and is not shown here.

No wrong acts, and too many asks

Both rejections were multi-line Bash scripts rated medium. P(allow) was 0.78 and 0.59 against a 0.85 threshold, so the gate asked. Of the 48 needless asks, 42 were agent-messaging calls such as adding a reaction or posting no reply. The rater called those high risk because the synthetic training data never contained them. That is a rater error, and the clearest thing to fix.

With only 2 rejections, the catch rate cannot be estimated, and always-allow would have had better log loss here (0.08 vs 0.29). Shadow mode shows the gate is not dangerous on this traffic. It does not yet show how well it catches bad actions.

What this does not show yet

The safety side holds on clean tests, and the cost is that it still asks too often. What is left is mostly the risk rater being over-cautious, not the decision model.

  • Synthetic data

    Training data and the main tests are LLM-written personas and actions. The 0.85 to 0.89 oracle ceiling shows the labels are noisy, and real people may not decide like the personas.

  • Little real evidence

    Shadow mode is one person, three weeks, 130 decisions and 2 rejections, scored with the earlier rater. That supports "not dangerous on this traffic" and nothing stronger.

  • Clean tests are used once

    Both clean tests are now spent, so any further tuning needs a new one. Test 3 was also smaller than planned, 174 actions instead of about 270.

  • Still asks too often

    32.7% needless asks on the latest clean test, against 27.3% with perfect risk labels. The gap is the rater flagging harmless actions as high. On real data, most needless asks come from one rater blind spot.

  • Next step, the rater

    Find the few high-risk actions the rater still scores low, since they set its round-up threshold. Add agent-messaging tool calls to the risk data, or rate them with a fixed rule.

  • Next step, more rejections

    Keep shadow mode running until the catch rate can be measured, and turn on per-user fitting with replay only once the test window has rejections.
